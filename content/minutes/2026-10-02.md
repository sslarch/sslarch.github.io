## Discussion about the direction of open science for qualitative methods

- Intro: Sophie Schmidt followed a talk at German Archaeological Theory Meeting about some of the adverse effects of open science for our discipline
    - Point raised: the open science movement has been pushed by stakeholders that pursue an own agenda. E.g. political and military actors have an interest in open science because they can get access to data for free without spending money on collection or without raising red flags 
    - This raises an ethical issue, which is: while nobody objects to the importance of transparency in terms of data/software publication, given that there is a dark side to it, where are the limits (if any) of open science? 
    - Secondly, it also puts a pressure onto the system because with open science favored, certain methods of research (qualitative especially) tend to lose importance.
- It is a good thing that reproducible work is supported, no doubt about that. But will there be space for qualitative, less reproducible approaches?
- There is to be said that much archaeological data is by its own nature not reproducible (excavations can't be re-excavated). We work with single data points that, while they can be categorized, offer little reproducibility.
    - A point raised during the conversation: beyond the possibility to reproduce statistical results, reproducibility can look different: consistency of findings and new results that support known evidence are being privileged to single studies that provide inconsistent conclusions, printed catalogues are a way of data sharing for typologies, ... .
- Some concerns can be raised: in situations where institutions cannot be trusted (for example nation states), and their relationships with the attribution of funding is fuzzy (nation states have a hand in deciding where the money goes), is the value of open science worth the risks of seeing science directed unwillingly?
- Where are the limits between Quantitative VS Qualitative, Subjective VS Objective, etc? There is an important aspect to be considered which is: who is controlling narratives?
    - Here the discussion goes much broader than open science. It has to do with power relationships, and the trust that everybody is going to "play the game" in the same way.
- To the point of fully open science: at what level are we held accountable for open science practices? How much data needs to be published under the principles of open science? E.g. should we publish excavation notebooks? Should we publish everything that we ever produced as scientists, including documents that may contain opinions, hot takes, known errors, etc?
    - point made: even in open science, we are not publishing sensitive information (detailed site locations, Indigenous information, etc.) and an overload of un-structured information may not be helpful to other scientists.
